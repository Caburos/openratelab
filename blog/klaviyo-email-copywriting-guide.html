KLAVIYO EMAIL COPYWRITING:
WHAT ACTUALLY MAKES
SUBSCRIBERS CLICK.

Good flow architecture with weak copy still underperforms. The words are not decoration on top of the strategy: they're the mechanism that converts a subject line into an open and an open into a sale.

UROS KORENE Founder, OpenRateLab

Copywriting is the service we lead with, and for good reason: it's the layer that decides whether everything else works. A perfectly built flow architecture with a clean segmentation system still lives or dies on whether the email gets opened and whether the words inside it move someone to buy.

This isn't a list of tricks. It's the actual thinking we apply to every subject line, every email body, and every CTA on active accounts.

The Subject Line's Only Job Is the Open

A subject line doesn't have to sell the product. It has to earn the open. Those are different jobs, and conflating them is the most common subject line mistake we see. Trying to cram the offer, the urgency, and the brand voice into 40 characters produces a subject line that does none of those things well.

Weaker "🔥 24 HOURS ONLY: Save 20% on Everything, Don't Miss Out!!"

Stronger "the discount ends tomorrow"

The second version reads like a person, not a promotion. It states one fact. It creates urgency without shouting. In our experience, specificity and restraint consistently beat volume and emoji-stacking, especially for lists that have been over-marketed to.

Structure Follows Intent, Not a Template

How long an email should be depends entirely on how much convincing the message requires. This is the single most misapplied rule in email copywriting: brands treat "short emails convert better" as a universal law, when it's actually intent-dependent.

  • High-intent moments (abandoned cart, back in stock, price drop): The subscriber already wants the product. Get out of the way. Show the item, remove friction, one clear CTA. Long copy here reads as an obstacle, not persuasion.
  • Low-intent moments (a cold campaign, a new product launch, an educational send): The subscriber needs a reason to care before they need a reason to buy. This is where a real story, a specific proof point, or an objection-handled explanation earns its length.

Matching structure to intent is why the same brand can run a 40-word abandoned cart email and a 400-word campaign in the same week, both converting well. They're solving different problems.

One CTA, One Job

Every email should be trying to get the reader to do exactly one thing. Repeating the same CTA multiple times in one email (above the fold, mid-body, at the bottom) is fine and often helps. Presenting two different CTAs (shop now vs. read our story, or buy vs. refer a friend) splits attention and measurably lowers conversion on both.

If a single email is being asked to sell a product, promote a referral program, and drive a review, it's not one email. It's three emails that got merged out of a scheduling convenience, and it will underperform all three individually.

Write for the Person, Not the Brand Voice Guide

Brand voice matters, but it's a filter, not a starting point. The starting point is always: what does this specific subscriber, at this specific moment in their relationship with the brand, actually need to hear? A welcome email talks to someone who just met you. A win-back email talks to someone who's drifting away. Using the same tone and structure for both ignores where the reader actually is.

This is also where data changes the copy. Across the accounts we manage, we've generated $1.2M+ in attributed email revenue while sustaining a 33%+ average open rate against a 25-30% e-commerce industry benchmark. That gap doesn't come from a cleverer brand voice. It comes from writing to the moment instead of writing to a template.

Editing Is the Real Skill

The first draft of most emails is too long, too hedged, and too polite. Editing is where copy earns its click-through rate: cutting qualifiers ("we think," "maybe," "could potentially"), cutting redundant sentences that restate the same point, and cutting anything that doesn't move the reader closer to the CTA.

A useful test: read the email out loud. Every sentence that makes you want to skim is a sentence a subscriber will actually skim. Cut it or sharpen it.

What Actually Makes a Klaviyo Email Convert?

Not a formula. A combination of: a subject line that earns the open without over-promising, a structure matched to how much convincing the moment actually requires, one clear CTA, and language that sounds like it was written for the specific person reading it, not broadcast to a list. Every account we manage gets this same scrutiny, whether it's a 40-word flow email or a 400-word campaign.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

What makes a good Klaviyo subject line? [+]

Specificity beats cleverness. A subject line naming a real detail (a product, a number, a deadline) consistently outperforms a vague or punny one. We test subject lines in pairs, not as a batch: one variable changed at a time, so the result actually tells you something. Curiosity works, but only when it's paired with relevance; curiosity alone reads as clickbait and trains subscribers to stop opening.

Should Klaviyo emails be short or long? [+]

Long enough to make the case, short enough that nothing is padding. Flows earning intent (abandoned cart, back in stock) can be short: the subscriber already wants the product, so get out of the way. Campaigns introducing a new idea or objection-handling a purchase decision need more room. The length should be a function of how much convincing the message actually requires, not a template.

How many CTAs should an email have? [+]

One job per email. Multiple CTAs pointing to the same action (shop now, above and below the fold) are fine. Multiple CTAs pointing to different actions split attention and lower conversion on both. If an email is trying to sell a product and get a review and promote a referral program, it's three emails wearing one email's clothes.
