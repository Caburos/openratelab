A sudden, unexplained drop in email open rate or inbox placement sends most teams straight into their own account looking for what broke. That's the right instinct about half the time. The other half, nothing on your end changed at all: an inbox provider updated how it filters mail, and every sender in a similar position took the same hit on the same day.

Those two causes need completely different fixes, and the fastest way to waste a week is to start "fixing" your flows, your sending domain, or your list hygiene when the actual cause was never inside your account to begin with.

## The Same Symptom, Two Different Causes

A dropping open rate looks identical whether the cause is on your end or the provider's end. The number goes down, the dashboard doesn't explain why, and the instinct is to start checking the things you control: recent template changes, segmentation, send frequency, list growth. That instinct makes sense, but it skips the one question that actually narrows down where to look first: did this happen to just you, or did it happen to everyone sending from a similar setup at roughly the same time?

## The First Check: Is It Just You, Or Is It Everyone

Before touching anything in your account, check whether other senders are reporting the same thing on the same day. Klaviyo and email-marketing communities tend to light up fast when a major provider changes its filtering behavior, because thousands of senders notice the same symptom within hours of each other. If you're seeing chatter from senders on different ESPs, different sending domains, and different list sizes all describing the same sudden drop, that's a strong signal the cause sits with the inbox provider, not with any individual account.

If you search and find nothing, that's also informative. A problem that's isolated to you, with no one else reporting anything similar, points toward something sender-side: an authentication gap, a reputation issue, or a change you made recently that you haven't connected to the drop yet.

## ISP-Side Signals: What A Platform-Wide Change Looks Like

An ISP-side filtering change tends to have a specific signature. It lands hard and uniformly, usually affecting every campaign sent that day rather than ramping up gradually. It isn't preceded by anything unusual on your end: no recent domain change, no volume spike, no new flow. And it tends to recover on its own within days as the provider tunes the new filtering behavior or as your account's existing reputation reasserts itself under the new rules.

This is also where [Gmail's phishing classifier misfiring on legitimate marketing email](/blog/gmail-phishing-flag-marketing-email) is worth checking specifically. Gmail has flagged properly authenticated marketing mail as a phishing risk before, independent of any actual sender-reputation problem, and it shows up exactly like this: a sudden, same-day drop with nothing in your own sending history to explain it.

## Sender-Side Signals: What Actually Changed On Your End

A sender-side cause almost always has a trigger, even if it isn't obvious at first glance. A recent increase in send volume, a new sending domain that hasn't built reputation yet, a content or template change, or a fast burst of list growth from a promotion can all shift how inbox providers treat your mail. Shared infrastructure matters here too: if [your sending IP is shared with other senders](/blog/shared-ip-reputation-klaviyo), someone else's bad sending behavior on that same IP pool can drag your placement down without you having changed anything yourself.

The tell that separates this from an ISP-side change is timing. A sender-side reputation problem usually doesn't appear as a clean, one-day cliff. It builds under the surface over several sends as reputation degrades, and what looks like a sudden drop is often the point where the degradation finally crossed a threshold a provider's filter acts on.

## What To Actually Check First, Not Guess

Open rate alone can't tell you which folder your mail landed in. A seed-list or inbox-placement test can: send to a set of test addresses across major providers and check manually where each one actually landed, inbox, Promotions, or spam. That single check does more to separate "placement problem" from "content problem" than any amount of staring at the open-rate trendline.

Alongside that, check your ESP's own status page for any reported delivery issues, and search for other senders reporting the same symptom on the same day. Both checks take a few minutes and rule out, or confirm, the ISP-side explanation before you start changing anything that took weeks to build.

## What This Doesn't Fix

A single "was it the ISP" check is a fast triage step, not a full diagnosis. If the drop isn't isolated to one bad day and keeps recurring, or if the seed-list test shows a genuine, worsening placement problem, [that's a sign of a deeper reputation collapse that follows its own diagnosable sequence](/blog/deliverability-collapse-order), and it needs the full authentication-to-complaints diagnostic, not just a one-time sanity check against the provider.
