A Klaviyo segmentation strategy that earns revenue is short, not clever. Most accounts end up with dozens of audiences, and a handful of them do all the work.

The segments that matter are built on behavior, mainly what people bought and how recently they engaged, and each one exists to change a decision about who gets more email, who gets less, and who gets a different offer. If a segment doesn't change a send, it is clutter.

## Why Behavior-Based Segments Beat A Long List Of Audiences

Behavior-based segments win because they predict what a person will do next better than who they are does. Someone who bought twice in the last quarter and someone who joined last year and never purchased are different audiences, whatever their age or location says.

It also helps to be clear on the mechanics. A list is static: people join it and stay until removed. A segment is dynamic: it is defined by conditions, so people move in and out automatically as they buy, open, click, or go quiet. For anything tied to revenue, build on segments. The audience stays accurate without anyone maintaining it.

## The Five Segments To Build First

These five cover the main send decisions a DTC brand makes. Build them in this order.

**Recently engaged.** People who opened or clicked within a window you choose, such as the last 90 days. This is your default send audience. Sending to it more often is usually safe, and it protects sender reputation because you are writing to people who still respond.

**Recent purchasers.** People who ordered in the last few weeks. The use here is mostly exclusion. Someone who just bought should not receive a promotion for the product they just bought, and they are better served by post-purchase content than a discount.

**Repeat customers.** People with more than one order. They already trust you, so they rarely need a discount to buy again, and they respond to early access, new arrivals, and replenishment reminders better than to a blanket offer.

**High-value customers.** People who rank at the top by order value or purchase count. Define the threshold from your own data instead of copying a number from an article. This group gets the best treatment: first access, a tone that acknowledges the relationship, and exclusion from broad discount sends that would train them to wait for a deal.

**Lapsed buyers.** People who bought before and haven't engaged or purchased in a long while. They are your most recoverable audience, which is why this segment feeds a [win-back flow](/blog/klaviyo-win-back-flow) before anyone gets suppressed. The same segment logic sits underneath the tiers we use for [list hygiene in Klaviyo](/blog/email-list-hygiene-klaviyo), where the goal is deliverability instead of revenue.

## What Each Segment Is Actually For

Every one of the five exists to answer a send question, and that is the test for any segment you add later.

Recently engaged answers who gets the regular send schedule. Recent purchasers answers who gets left out of a promotion. Repeat customers answers who doesn't need a discount. High-value customers answers who gets the best experience and who is protected from over-discounting. Lapsed buyers answers who gets a win-back attempt before suppression.

When you build a new segment, write the sentence "for these people we will do X differently" before you build it. If you can't finish the sentence, don't build the segment.

## Common Segmentation Mistakes

The most common mistake is building segments nobody sends to. They pile up, they get named inconsistently, and eventually nobody trusts the account enough to know which ones are safe to use.

A second mistake is overlapping segments with no clear purpose, such as three slightly different definitions of "engaged" used in different places. Pick one definition, document it, and reuse it. A third is using segmentation to avoid fixing the email itself. If a campaign underperforms, the answer is rarely a narrower audience. It is usually the offer, the copy, or the timing, and splitting the audience just hides the problem in smaller numbers.

## How To Check A Segment Is Earning Its Place

A segment earns its place when two things are true: you can name the send decision it changed, and the revenue per recipient for sends to it justifies the effort.

Compare revenue per recipient for campaigns sent to the segment against the same campaigns sent to the whole list, which is the same kind of split that makes [flow revenue and campaign revenue](/blog/flow-vs-campaign-revenue) worth tracking separately. If a segment doesn't change what you send or doesn't change the result, retire it. A smaller account that everyone understands is easier to run than a larger one nobody dares to touch.
