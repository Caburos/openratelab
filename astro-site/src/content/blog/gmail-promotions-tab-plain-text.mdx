A welcome email kept landing in Gmail's Promotions tab. Five times. The plain-text version of the same message hit Primary on the first try. That gap says less about the copy and more about what Gmail's classifier is actually reading.

## What Actually Drives The Promotions Classification

Gmail's tab sorting weighs formatting and engagement signals far more heavily than the literal content of an email. Heavy HTML structure, a high image-to-text ratio, and marketing-style layout patterns all read as promotional, regardless of how personal or warm the copy itself sounds.

Engagement history with the sender matters too. A recipient who has never opened anything from a domain gives Gmail less reason to trust the next send lands somewhere important, which compounds the formatting signal rather than offsetting it.

None of these signals are published as an official checklist. Gmail doesn't document the exact classifier logic, so most of what's known comes from testing and observed patterns like this one, not a confirmed spec. Treat the guidance here as a strong, repeatedly observed pattern, not a guaranteed rule.

## Why Welcome Emails Are Especially Exposed

A welcome email is usually the most heavily templated, most on-brand email in an entire flow. Full header image, brand colors, multiple content blocks, a polished layout built to make a strong first impression.

That's exactly the pattern Gmail's classifier is tuned to catch. The very design choices meant to impress a new subscriber are the same choices that push the email toward Promotions in the first place.

It's also the worst place in the flow for this to happen. A welcome email is the first real signal a new subscriber gets about whether the relationship is worth paying attention to, and a delayed or buried first message undercuts everything the rest of the flow is trying to build.

## The Plain-Text Test

Stripping the same message down to plain text removes the strongest signal pushing it toward Promotions. Less HTML structure, no image-heavy layout, nothing that visually matches the shape of a typical marketing send.

This isn't a guaranteed override. It's a strong lever specifically because it removes the clearest signal from the equation, not because plain text has some special status with Gmail's classifier.

A middle path exists too: a lightly styled email with minimal HTML, one or two small images at most, and a layout closer to a personal message than a campaign. That often captures most of the placement benefit while keeping more brand presence than fully stripped plain text.

## What This Means For Flow Design

The lesson isn't "send everything as plain text." Promotions isn't a penalty box. Most marketing content genuinely belongs there, and plenty of recipients check that tab deliberately.

The real question is which specific emails in a flow actually need Primary placement. [A welcome series](/blog/klaviyo-welcome-series-guide) is a strong candidate, since a delayed or missed first impression costs more than a routine campaign landing in Promotions instead. Order confirmations and other lifecycle moments people actively watch for are worth the same consideration.

Reserve the lighter-formatting approach for those specific emails, not the entire send calendar. A welcome email that lands in Primary and a promotional campaign that lands in Promotions are both doing exactly what they should.

## A Practical Check

Before assuming a placement problem is unfixable, test directly. Send the email to a handful of Gmail accounts you control and check which tab it actually lands in, rather than guessing from open rate alone.

If it's landing in Promotions and that's the wrong tab for that specific email, try a lighter version first: less HTML, fewer images, a simpler layout closer to plain text. [Authentication issues](/blog/email-deliverability-guide-dtc) can compound placement problems too, so confirm SPF and DKIM are clean before concluding formatting alone is the cause.
