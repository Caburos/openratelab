Most teams treat a deliverability drop as one problem. It isn't. It's a sequence, and the sequence runs in a specific order every time: authentication and sender reputation fail first, inbox placement drops second, engagement declines third, and complaints rise last, feeding back into the first stage and keeping the collapse going. Diagnosing a deliverability collapse means figuring out which stage you're actually in, not reacting to whichever symptom happened to be the one you noticed.

That distinction matters because the fixes for each stage are different, and a fix aimed at a late-stage symptom does nothing if an earlier stage is still broken underneath it.

## The Four-Stage Collapse

Each stage is a direct cause of the one after it. Authentication problems cause inbox placement problems. Inbox placement problems cause engagement problems. Engagement problems cause complaint problems. And complaints feed straight back into sender reputation, which is stage one again, now worse than when the collapse started.

Knowing the order means you can work backward from whatever symptom you noticed to the stage that's actually broken, instead of fixing the symptom and watching it come back.

## Stage 1: Authentication And Sender Reputation

This is the root cause stage, and it's almost always invisible from inside a campaign dashboard. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC alignment either hold or they don't, and when they don't, inbox providers start treating every message from that domain as lower trust, regardless of content or send history. The same applies to IP and domain reputation: a shared sending IP that picked up a bad history before you started using it, or a domain that moved providers without a clean warmup, starts this same decline before a single campaign metric moves.

The fix at this stage is mechanical, not creative: audit SPF, DKIM, and DMARC against the live DNS records, not against what the platform dashboard claims, and confirm the sending domain and IP don't carry reputation damage from before you controlled them.

## Stage 2: Inbox Placement

This is the first stage that produces a symptom anyone notices, and it's usually misread as a content problem. Messages that pass basic spam filtering but carry weak authentication or early reputation damage don't get rejected outright. They get placed: into spam, into the promotions tab, into a lower-priority inbox tier that the recipient checks less often. The message delivered successfully by every technical measure, but it landed somewhere that behaves like it didn't.

Placement problems look like a sudden drop in opens with no corresponding change in list size or send volume, which is the tell that separates this stage from a genuine engagement problem: the audience didn't change, where the message landed did.

## Stage 3: Engagement Decline

Once placement degrades, engagement declines as a direct consequence, not as an independent event. Fewer people see the message in a primary inbox, so fewer people open it, and fewer people who open it click. This is the stage most teams actually diagnose, because it's the one visible in every standard reporting dashboard: open rate, click rate, click-to-open rate, all trending down together.

The mistake at this stage is treating it as the actual problem and rewriting subject lines or redesigning templates to fix it. Content changes can improve engagement when the mail is landing in the inbox. They do very little when the mail is landing in spam, because the content never gets evaluated by the fraction of the audience who would have engaged with it.

## Stage 4: Complaints And The Reputation Loop

This is the stage that turns a temporary dip into a sustained collapse. As engagement declines, the subscribers who remain engaged enough to act are more likely to be the ones who are frustrated rather than interested, and spam complaint rates climb even as overall open rates fall. Those complaints get read by inbox providers as a fresh reputation signal, which damages the sender reputation that stage one depends on, which restarts the cycle at a worse starting point than before.

This is also why a collapse left undiagnosed tends to get worse over time rather than plateauing. Each cycle through the loop adds more reputation damage than the last, because the base of genuinely engaged subscribers who would have offset the complaint signal keeps shrinking.

## Diagnose Before You Fix

The practical value of knowing this order is that it tells you where to look first. A dropping open rate by itself doesn't tell you whether you're in stage two, three, or already cycling through stage four. Checking authentication and recent sending-infrastructure changes first rules stage one in or out quickly, and it's the one stage that, left unfixed, makes every downstream fix temporary at best.

If [your DMARC setup has gaps at the seam between your email platform and your DNS host](/blog/dmarc-multi-vendor-dns), that's a stage-one problem hiding behind what looks like a stage-three symptom. The same is true for [SPF and DKIM configuration more broadly](/blog/email-deliverability-guide-dtc): these aren't one-time setup tasks, they're the first thing worth re-checking whenever a deliverability number moves, before touching subject lines, send frequency, or list segmentation.
