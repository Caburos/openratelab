Klaviyo Composer launched on March 24, 2026, as part of a wider expansion of Klaviyo's AI agents. Most of what's been written about it covers the same use case: describe a campaign in plain language, and Composer generates it, segments, messaging, and all. That's real, but it's not the half of Klaviyo Composer worth paying attention to if your flows are already built.

The less-publicized capability is that Composer also works on existing automations. Point it at a flow you already have running, and it can tell you where customers are dropping out of it.

## What Klaviyo Composer Actually Does

The core function is prompt-to-campaign generation. Describe a goal in everyday language, and Composer produces a launch-ready campaign: audience segments and messaging, optimized across channels, grounded in the account's own customer data rather than generic templates. Klaviyo's own materials frame this as campaigns that used to take days now generated in minutes.

The part that matters more for an account that already has flows running is the second capability: Composer can also be pointed at an existing automation and asked where it's losing customers. It identifies what's causing the drop-off, not just that one exists.

Either way, nothing ships without a human approving it. Composer surfaces a draft or a diagnosis. It doesn't publish anything on its own.

## The Campaign-Generation Use Case Gets All The Attention

It's easy to see why. "Describe a campaign and watch it get built" is the more demonstrable, more screenshot-friendly capability, and it's genuinely useful for an account starting from nothing or testing an idea fast. It also makes for a better product announcement: a tool that writes a finished campaign in minutes is a cleaner headline than a tool that tells you one of your existing emails is quietly underperforming.

But if your Klaviyo account already has a welcome series, an abandoned cart flow, and a win-back sequence live, campaign generation isn't solving your actual bottleneck. You don't need more content generated faster. You need to know which of the flows you already have is quietly underperforming, and why. Writing a brand-new campaign doesn't fix a broken step three emails into your abandoned cart flow. It just adds more content on top of a problem nobody's looked at yet.

## Why Flow Diagnosis Matters More Once You're Already Set Up

A flow that's losing people somewhere in its sequence usually doesn't announce itself. Revenue from that flow drifts down slowly enough that it reads as normal variance until someone actually goes looking, and most teams only go looking when a quarterly report forces the question. By the time a drop shows up in a quarterly number, it's already been running for months, usually unnoticed because the flow as a whole still produces some revenue. A blended number can look fine while one specific step is actively losing people.

Pointing Composer at an existing flow and asking where it loses customers turns that into something you can check on demand instead of waiting to notice. It's the same instinct behind structuring a [Klaviyo flow revenue report separately from campaign revenue](/blog/flow-vs-campaign-revenue): you can't fix what you're not specifically looking at, and a single blended revenue number hides exactly this kind of flow-level problem. It's also the same work behind [one account's jump to a 70.7% open rate after a full flow rebuild](/case-studies/ecommerce-open-rate-system): the gain came from finding specifically where the existing flows were losing people, not from generating new content.

## What Composer's Diagnosis Still Can't Replace

Composer can point at where in a sequence people are dropping off and surface a plausible reason. Whether that reason is the actual reason, and what the right fix is for this specific brand's purchase cycle, margin, and customer behavior, is still a judgment call. A model flagging "email two has a high drop-off rate" doesn't know whether that's because the offer is weak, the timing is wrong relative to how this brand's customers actually browse, or the segment it's going to shouldn't have gotten that email at all.

That gap between a flagged symptom and the right fix is exactly where [Klaviyo's other AI features already fall short](/blog/klaviyo-ai-features): they're real, useful inputs, not finished decisions. Composer's flow-diagnosis capability is the same shape of tool. It narrows down where to look. It doesn't replace someone who understands the account deciding what to actually change.

## What To Actually Try This Week

If you've only used Klaviyo Composer to generate a campaign, or haven't used it at all, the higher-value first test is the one almost nobody runs: pick your most important existing flow (welcome series or abandoned cart are the usual candidates) and ask Composer directly where it's losing people.

Whatever it surfaces, treat it as a lead worth checking, not a verdict. Pull the actual step-by-step conversion data for that flow yourself, confirm whether Composer's flagged point matches what the numbers show, and only then decide what to change. If the flagged step is the second or third email in the sequence, check what that email is actually asking the subscriber to do and whether it matches where they realistically are in considering the purchase. A lot of mid-flow drop-off traces back to an email asking for a decision the subscriber isn't ready to make yet, not a technical or deliverability problem Composer would ever be positioned to catch.

Do this for one flow before doing it for all of them. The value of the exercise is in actually following through on what it surfaces, not in running it across every flow at once and ending up with five diagnoses and no time to act on any of them.
