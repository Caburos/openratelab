When your email bounce rate keeps climbing after you have cleaned the list, the old addresses were never the real problem. Cleaning removes addresses that have already gone bad. It does nothing about the new bad addresses that arrive every week.

Think of the list as a stock and a flow. The clean fixed the stock, and the bounces you are seeing now come from the flow, which is where to look.

## Why A Rising Email Bounce Rate Survives A Clean

A list clean is a one-time subtraction, so any steady source of bad addresses refills the problem within a few sends. If a signup form, an import or an integration keeps adding addresses that cannot receive mail, the bounce rate rebuilds itself no matter how carefully you cleaned.

This is also why the usual engagement-based cleanup, which we cover in our guide to [email list hygiene in Klaviyo](/blog/email-list-hygiene-klaviyo), does not fix this on its own. That approach deals with people who stopped reading. A bounce is a different failure: the message never arrived at all.

## Split Hard Bounces From Soft Bounces First

Hard bounces are permanent failures, usually an address that does not exist, while soft bounces are temporary, such as a full inbox or a receiving server that was briefly down. The two point at different causes, so separate them before you change anything.

A rise in hard bounces means bad addresses are getting onto your list. A rise in soft bounces across many unrelated addresses can mean a mailbox provider is deferring your mail, which points at sender reputation and sits outside the list itself. Most sending platforms, Klaviyo included, stop sending to addresses that hard bounce, but labels and exact rules vary by version, so check how your account reports and suppresses each type.

## Find The Source Of The New Bad Addresses

Group the bounced contacts by how and when they joined, because the pattern usually names the culprit within minutes. Build a segment of recently bounced profiles, then compare them against the properties you already store: signup form, source, join date, and whether they came from an import or an integration.

Look for three things. Do the bounces cluster on one form? Do they cluster on one date range, such as the week of a giveaway or a discount popup launch? Do they cluster on one import file? Whichever one holds most of the bounces is where to work first, and the rest can wait.

## The Usual Entry-Point Causes

Four causes account for most of what we see on DTC lists:

- **Typos at signup.** Domains like gmial or hotmial in the address field turn a real customer into a hard bounce.
- **Bot signups.** Scripts submit addresses to open forms, many of them fake or belonging to other people.
- **Incentive abuse.** A discount for signing up attracts people who enter a throwaway address to get the code.
- **Old imports.** A list uploaded from another tool or an older system carries years of decay with it.

## Fix It At The Door

Close the entry point rather than cleaning again afterwards. Depending on what your forms and platform offer, that can mean bot protection on the form, a confirmation step before an address joins the main list, a prompt that catches obvious domain typos, and a rule that new imports go to a small test segment before they get a full send.

For imports, send first to a small slice and watch what bounces before you mail the rest. If a slice bounces heavily, hold the whole file back. Our guide to [email deliverability for DTC brands](/blog/email-deliverability-guide-dtc) explains why a high bounce rate on one send can affect how mailbox providers treat the next ones.

## What To Watch Afterwards

Track the bounce rate per campaign and per signup source for several weeks, and compare against your own earlier sends instead of a benchmark from an article. You are looking for a flat line after the fix, and for new subscribers whose first send does not bounce.
