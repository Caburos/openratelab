Most advice about hiring an email marketing agency tells you what to ask once you're already on a call. The more useful warning signs show up earlier than that: in how an agency behaves during the sales process itself, before you've asked anything at all.

Red flags hiring an email marketing agency are easier to miss than good questions are to prepare, because a bad agency rarely says anything false. They just skip the steps a good one wouldn't skip, and brands shopping for a Klaviyo partner don't always notice what's missing until the contract is already signed.

## Red Flag: No Audit Before A Price

A number before an audit is the first and clearest warning sign. Pricing for email marketing work depends on list size, current send volume, how many flows already exist, and how much cleanup the account needs before new work can start. An agency that quotes a flat monthly rate without asking about any of that is either guessing, or planning to discover the real scope after you've signed and renegotiate from there.

A short audit, even an informal one on a sales call, should surface specific findings: how many flows are live, what the current open and click rates look like, whether the list has hygiene problems. If the only thing offered before a price is a generic pitch deck, that's the gap to notice.

## Can't Tell You Who Actually Works On Your Account

The person on the sales call is often not the person who builds your flows or writes your copy. That's normal. What's a red flag is when an agency can't or won't name who specifically will be doing the work, what their role is, and how to reach them once you're signed.

This matters because [the questions that actually separate a good Klaviyo agency from a mediocre one](/blog/how-to-choose-a-klaviyo-agency) include asking directly who works on an account, not just who's selling it. An agency confident in its own team answers that specifically. One that's vague about it is often routing accounts to whoever's available, not whoever's right for the work.

## Templated Flows With No Segmentation Behind Them

Every Klaviyo account needs the same baseline flows: welcome, abandoned cart, post-purchase. The red flag isn't that an agency builds these, it's building them identically across every client with no segmentation strategy underneath. A welcome flow that doesn't change based on how a subscriber signed up, or an abandoned cart sequence that treats a first-time browser the same as a repeat customer, is a template with a client's logo on it, not a built-for-this-account system.

Ask specifically how flows get segmented for an account like yours. A real answer references your actual customer behavior and product catalog. A vague one describes Klaviyo's default flow categories back to you as if that were the strategy.

## No Clear Reporting Cadence

Reporting that only happens when you ask for it isn't reporting, it's a status update under pressure. A real cadence is agreed before signing: how often, in what format, and broken down by what. Flow revenue separated from campaign revenue matters here specifically, since [those two numbers answer different questions and get muddled together far too often](/blog/flow-vs-campaign-revenue) when an agency isn't tracking them distinctly.

If an agency can't describe what a typical monthly report looks like when you ask during the sales process, that's usually because no consistent format exists yet, and you'd be the one building the habit of asking for it after the fact.

## Long Lock-In With No Trial Period

A 12-month contract with no exit clause and no initial trial period shifts all the risk onto the brand before any results exist. A confident agency doesn't need that structure. Month-to-month terms, or a short initial period before a longer commitment, signal that the agency expects the work itself to be the reason you stay, not the contract.

This doesn't mean every long-term agreement is a red flag; established relationships often run on longer terms once trust is built, the same way [a properly paced multi-month ramp can still produce a real revenue jump](/case-studies/dtc-brand-multi-month-ramp) without requiring a long contract to get there. The warning sign is specifically a long lock-in offered at the very start, before either side has anything to point to.

## Generic Revenue-Lift Promises

"We'll grow your email revenue" said with no baseline is marketing, not a forecast. A real projection references your current numbers: today's email revenue share, current flow performance, list size and growth rate, then explains what specifically would move those. An agency promising a flat percentage lift applied to every prospective client regardless of their starting point hasn't actually looked at the account yet.

This is the inverse of the audit-first point above, and it shows up for the same reason: promises made before diagnosis are promises made about nobody's account in particular.

## What A Clean Process Actually Looks Like Instead

None of this means every agency showing one of these signs is a bad fit, context matters, and a single ambiguous answer on a first call isn't disqualifying on its own. What's worth noticing is the pattern: an audit before a price, a named team before a signature, segmentation specific to your product before templated flows, a reporting cadence agreed upfront, contract terms that don't require blind trust, and a growth estimate tied to your actual numbers.
