A Klaviyo signup form trigger is the rule that decides when a form appears, and most stores set one rule and apply it to every page. That is the most common reason a signup popup underperforms: the timing that suits a homepage visitor is wrong for someone about to buy.

The fix is to choose the trigger by what the visitor is doing on that page. Browsing pages get a soft, delayed ask. Decision pages get little or nothing. Content pages get an offer that matches what the person came to read.

## Why One Klaviyo Signup Form Trigger For Every Page Underperforms

One popup everywhere ignores intent. A visitor landing on the homepage, a shopper comparing two products, and a reader halfway through a blog post are in three different mindsets, and the same overlay with the same timer treats them as one.

The cost shows up in two ways. Where the timing is too early, people close the form without reading it, which wastes the view and annoys a visitor you may want later. Where the timing is too late or too narrow, the form never appears for people who would have signed up. Splitting the setup by page type fixes both without adding any extra traffic.

## Homepage And Collection Pages: A Soft Ask After They Have Looked

On homepage and collection pages, a short delay or a scroll trigger works best, because these visitors are browsing and a form that appears after they have seen the products reads as an offer, not an interruption.

Keep the ask simple here: the incentive, one field, one button. These pages bring the most new visitors, so this is where a form usually earns most of its signups. The exact option names inside Klaviyo's form builder change between versions, but delay, scroll depth, and page targeting by URL have been consistent features of the form targeting settings.

## Product Pages: Protect The Purchase

On product pages, delay the form or limit it to exit intent on desktop. A visitor reading specifications or checking sizing is close to a decision, and a popup that covers the page right then competes with the add-to-cart button.

If you want a signup here, a less intrusive format such as a small flyout or an embedded form lower on the page does the job without blocking anything. The visitor who is not ready to buy today is exactly who you want on the list, so give them an easy way in that does not get in the way of the visitor who is ready.

## Cart And Checkout: Leave The Popup Off

On cart and checkout pages, the best setting is usually off. Every second of attention there should go to completing the order, and a signup overlay adds a decision at the worst possible moment.

This is also where your abandoned cart flow takes over: shoppers who reach checkout and identify themselves with an email are already covered by that sequence. A new popup on top of it adds friction and, at best, collects an address you were about to get anyway.

## Blog And Content Pages: Scroll-Based, Offer Matched To The Article

On content pages, a scroll-based trigger tied to a relevant offer feels natural, because the reader has shown interest by reading. A form that appears after most of the article has been read can offer something related, such as a guide, a checklist, or a discount on the product the article discusses.

A generic "10% off" popup on a how-to post is the weakest version of this. If the offer matches the content, the form is closer to a useful next step than an ad. This is also the group of subscribers most likely to engage with the [welcome series](/blog/klaviyo-welcome-series-guide) that follows, so it is worth tagging them by source.

## Mobile Needs Its Own Rules

On a phone, avoid full-screen overlays that appear immediately and use a delayed trigger, a scroll trigger, or a banner or flyout that leaves the content visible. Exit intent does not translate to touch screens the way it does to a mouse, so do not rely on it there.

Check the form on a real phone before publishing, with a close button that is easy to tap and a form that does not cover the page the moment someone arrives. A popup that blocks the page for a visitor who arrived from search is the pattern worth avoiding.

## How To Check A Signup Form Is Earning Its Place

A form earns its place when its signup rate holds up and the people it brings in engage. Check signup rate per form (submissions divided by views), not overall list growth, and change one thing at a time so a result has a cause.

Then look past the count. A form that adds many subscribers who never open the welcome email is adding to your [list hygiene](/blog/email-list-hygiene-klaviyo) workload, not to your revenue. If you tag subscribers by the form they came from, you can later see which sources produce buyers, and the same breakdown feeds the [segments worth building first](/blog/klaviyo-segmentation-strategy).
