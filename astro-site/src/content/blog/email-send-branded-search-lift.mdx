A send's open rate measures who read it. It says nothing about who closed the email, then opened a new tab and searched the brand name instead of clicking through.

## The Direct Response Isn't The Whole Response

Open rates and click rates measure interaction with the email itself. They're accurate for what they measure, and they're also incomplete by design.

Someone who reads a subject line, feels genuinely interested, and still doesn't click isn't necessarily uninterested. Some portion of that group closes the email and opens a browser tab to search the brand name directly, verifying the company or comparing it before deciding to buy. That search, and any resulting purchase, happens entirely outside the email platform's own tracking.

This secondary effect doesn't replace open and click data. It sits alongside it, measuring something the primary metrics were never built to see.

## What A Branded-Search Lift Actually Looks Like

The pattern to look for is a repeated, consistent bump in branded search volume on or immediately after send days, not explainable by any other campaign running at the same time.

This effect is strongest for higher-consideration or higher-price-point products. A reader deciding whether to spend real money has a reason to verify the brand before clicking straight into a cart. A low-consideration, low-price impulse purchase rarely produces this pattern, since there's little reason to research before a small, low-risk decision.

A single spike after one send proves nothing on its own. The pattern needs to repeat across multiple sends before it's a real signal rather than a coincidence with some unrelated search trend.

## How To Actually Check For It

Google Search Console is the tool for this, not anything inside Klaviyo's own reporting. Pull branded search impressions and clicks for the brand name and its close variants (common misspellings, "[brand] reviews," "[brand] login") over a period spanning several sends.

Compare search volume on send days and the day immediately after against a baseline built from non-send days in the same window. Control for anything else that could explain a spike: a paid ad flight running at the same time, a press mention, a seasonal demand pattern specific to the product category.

If a consistent bump shows up specifically tied to send timing and nothing else explains it, that's a real secondary effect worth factoring into how a send's actual value gets judged, beyond what its own open and click numbers show.

## Why This Matters Most For Certain Kinds Of Sends

A send announcing a new product line, a significant price change, or a brand repositioning is more likely to prompt this kind of research-before-buying behavior than a routine promotional email a subscriber has seen a dozen times before.

If a brand runs higher-consideration campaigns and only ever judges them by click-through rate, it's measuring the easy half of the response and missing the half that happens in a search bar instead of an inbox.

## What Not To Conclude From A Correlation

A correlation between send timing and a search bump isn't proof the email caused it, especially with a small number of data points. Before treating this as confirmed, check that the pattern holds across enough sends to rule out coincidence, and that nothing else running at the same time offers a simpler explanation.

Treat an apparent branded-search lift as a hypothesis worth tracking over more sends, not a settled fact after one or two data points.
