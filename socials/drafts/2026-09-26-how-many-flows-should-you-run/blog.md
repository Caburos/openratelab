"How many flows should I be running?" is the wrong first question. A brand with 12 active flows and real gaps in core coverage is worse off than one running 6 that actually map to the customer journey.

## Why Count Isn't The Right Metric

Flow count is easy to compare and easy to obsess over, which is exactly why it's the wrong thing to optimize. Two accounts can both run 8 flows and be in completely different positions: one covering every core lifecycle moment cleanly, the other duplicating a win-back sequence three different ways while never building browse abandonment at all.

The real question isn't how many flows exist. It's which customer moments those flows actually cover, and whether anything important is missing.

This matters more than it sounds, because flow count is often the first thing a new team member or an outside consultant asks about, and answering it directly invites the wrong kind of comparison. A higher number reads as more sophisticated even when it isn't.

## The Core Coverage Checklist

Most DTC brands need roughly 5 to 7 flows to cover the moments that drive the bulk of automated revenue: a welcome series for new subscribers, abandoned cart for people who added to cart and left, browse abandonment for people who viewed but didn't add to cart, post-purchase for people who just bought, and a win-back or sunset sequence for people who've gone quiet.

That's the baseline, not a ceiling. A brand with a genuinely distinct product line, subscription model, or repeat-purchase pattern might need one or two more to cover something specific to how their customers actually buy. [The full setup guide](/blog/klaviyo-email-flows-guide) covers each of these in structural detail.

A brand selling a genuine one-time purchase, a piece of furniture, a large appliance, has a real case for skipping a heavy post-purchase repeat-buy sequence in favor of a review and referral-focused version instead. The baseline is a starting point to adapt from, not a fixed list to copy blind.

## Where Accounts Commonly Over-Build

Multiple near-duplicate win-back variants are the most common form of over-building. An account runs three or four slightly different re-engagement sequences targeting overlapping segments, none of them clearly distinct from the others, none of them clearly outperforming a single well-built version.

Hyper-segmentation does the same damage in a different shape. Splitting an already-small audience into narrow segments, each with its own flow variant, often means every version gets too little volume to actually perform or teach you anything reliable.

## Where Accounts Commonly Under-Build

Browse abandonment and post-purchase are the two flows most often missing entirely. Browse abandonment gets skipped because it feels less urgent than cart abandonment, even though it catches genuine interest earlier in the funnel, before a cart is ever started.

Post-purchase gets skipped because attention naturally moves on once the sale closes. That's exactly why it's a missed opportunity: it's one of the highest-leverage windows for driving a repeat purchase or a review, and most accounts simply never build for it.

Both gaps share the same underlying pattern: the missing flow targets a moment that doesn't feel like an emergency the way an abandoned cart does, so it keeps getting deprioritized in favor of whatever feels more urgent that week.

## A Simple Coverage Audit

List every currently active flow next to the specific customer moment it's meant to cover. Anything genuinely covered gets checked off. Anything duplicated gets flagged for consolidation. Anything missing from the core five to seven moments becomes the actual next flow to build, not whatever felt interesting that week.
